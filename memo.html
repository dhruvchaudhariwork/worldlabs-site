The thesis

Learn from what happens.

World Labs is building original training data and game-engine environments for AI agents: models that take actions to complete tasks. Game engines combine code, geometry, physics, and interacting systems in worlds we can run, reset, and inspect. This makes them useful for teaching an agent to plan, test its assumptions, and recover when an action fails. The immediate use is better agents for game development and interactive worlds.

Two concrete uses

Build a system.
Learn to operate in one.

The second use is training agents inside existing games: navigate to a destination, collect resources, or coordinate several actions despite incomplete information. Developers can supply varied worlds; AI labs can use them for training and evaluation. SIMA 2 demonstrates this route, combining human demonstrations with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks, using images and keyboard-and-mouse actions, and testing on games excluded from RL training. [5]

How training works

Turn consequences into feedback.

Each environment needs a repeatable starting state, a goal, tools for observing and acting, and a protected success check the agent cannot rewrite. In reinforcement learning (RL), the model attempts a task, receives a score, and a trainer updates the model to favor successful behavior. Resetting the world enables repeated practice; changing layouts, constraints, and failures discourages memorization. Subjective goals still need human review.

Our intended offering pairs these environments with original, rights-cleared code, scenes, demonstrations, and recorded attempts. Code and scenes can support pretraining, the broad learning before task-specific practice; demonstrations teach useful behavior; scored attempts support RL. The same environment can evaluate a model without updating it. These are distinct uses of a shared training infrastructure.

The evidence

A foundation we can test.

GameDevBench reports GPT-5.4 task success rising from 41.1% to 52.0% with screenshot and runtime-video feedback. That is better information during execution, not evidence of RL training. [1] OpenGameEval, GameEngineBench, and GameCraft-Bench reinforce the need to evaluate running scenes, integrated engine behavior, and complete playable systems. [2][3][4]

Actual training evidence is promising: Play to Generalize reports visual-game RL improving a model’s score on MathVista, a visual-reasoning test, from 68.0% to 71.9%; SPIRAL reports reasoning gains from strategic, language-based self-play. [6][7] These are specific transfer results, not proof that richer worlds always help. Procgen motivates varied training levels and unseen test levels to distinguish generalization from memorization. [8]

Beyond games

The engine is a starting point.

The expansion we see is into simulation and robotics research, including digital twins: virtual copies of physical places and systems. A warehouse simulation could train an agent to reroute around a blocked aisle, inspect equipment, or recover from a failed pickup. The reusable infrastructure is the same: worlds with persistent state, actions with consequences, resettable scenarios, and measurable outcomes. Transfer requires testing on the target workflow; simulation alone cannot establish physical safety or real-world performance. Our near-term focus remains game engines while we measure which capabilities travel beyond them.

The research plan

Prove the training value.

Our proposed study starts from the same model and compares generic coding tasks, game-engine tasks, and a mixture under matched training compute. Evaluation uses unseen projects and task families, identical tools, and equal execution budgets. We will measure completion, repeatability, recovery, broken existing behavior, and cost per successful task. A separate comparison will isolate visual feedback from training gains. The cited papers establish a research foundation; World Labs still needs its own controlled results. The product becomes valuable when these environments reliably teach useful capabilities at a competitive cost.
